The little challenge

Can you cover it?

A 4 × 4 board has its top-left and bottom-right corners removed. That leaves 14 squares.

You have 7 dominoes. Each covers exactly two squares that share an edge.

Can you cover every remaining square?

2 Count two kinds of squares

Every horizontal or vertical step changes color. So a domino always covers one dark and one light square.

DLOne of each. Every time.
Dark squares left6
Light squares left8

These counts track uncovered squares. Placing a domino reduces each by 1, so their difference stays 2.

3 Explain why no strategy can work
Start: 8 dark + 8 light

The two removed corners are both dark.

After removal: 6 dark + 8 light

But 7 dominoes would need 7 dark + 7 light. There aren’t enough dark squares.

Another way to see it: every domino takes away one of each, so the difference of 2 never changes. A fully covered board would have 0 dark and 0 light left—a difference of 0. That is impossible.

What if we remove one dark and one light square?

That leaves 31 of each, so this color-count argument no longer rules out a covering. Equal counts are necessary, but on a general board they are not sufficient: the shape and connectivity can still prevent a covering.

Go further · Possible or impossible?

Can we prove this board works?

Be a board detective. Predict first, then reveal the evidence. A failed test can prove “no”; passing a test only means “keep looking.”

3 Build a proof that it works

Each connected color is one domino. Step through the covering, or show it all at once.

The rule to remember

Unequal colors → impossible. Each domino removes one of each color.

Equal colors → investigate further. The counts pass one test, but isolated squares or disconnected regions can still block a covering.

A complete legal covering → possible. One example is enough to prove existence. Unsuccessful attempts alone do not prove impossibility.
