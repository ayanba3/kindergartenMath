Frogs trading places.

Can the two teams swap sides without moving backward?

The rules

Right frogs move right; left frogs move left. A frog can slide into the next empty pad or jump over exactly one frog into the empty pad beyond.

There are two teal right-facing frogs and two coral left-facing frogs. The middle pad starts empty.

→ teal frogs move right · ← coral frogs move left · ◯ dotted pad is empty. A jump crosses exactly one frog.

Available moves

The idea

Every right frog must pass every left frog: with n frogs on each side there are n² opposite-team crossings. A jump over your own team traps a pair, so a completed swap uses exactly those n² jumps. Each frog travels n+1 spaces, for 2n(n+1) spaces total. The jumps cover 2n² spaces, leaving 2n one-space slides. That gives n²+2n moves: 8 for n=2 and 15 for n=3. The worked route achieves the bound.

Why a same-team jump cannot be part of a completed swap

Suppose a right-facing frog jumps over another right-facing frog. The empty pad ends up to the left of an adjacent RR pair. The empty pad cannot get past both frogs: that would require a jump over two frogs, which is not allowed. So neither frog can move again. Looking backward before the jump gives a frozen RR pair with the hole on its right. The pair would have to be wholly in the starting R region before the jump and wholly in the finishing R region after it, but this jump shifts it only one pad. Those regions are separated by the other team, so that is impossible. The left-facing case is the mirror image.

Therefore a completed swap uses exactly the n² jumps over opposite-team pairs. The jumps cover 2n² of the total 2n(n+1) travel spaces, leaving 2n slides. That gives the exact minimum n²+2n.

Crossing pairs

For n frogs each side, the shortest count is n²+2n: n² crossings plus 2n slides. For three on each side: 9 jumps + 6 slides = 15.
